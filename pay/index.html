PAY OUR PROVIDERS

Child care is the work that makes all other work possible.

New York is building universal child care. But the providers who make it possible earn less than 96% of the state's workers, and New York City family child care providers take home a median of $6.01 an hour. Universal child care can't be built on poverty wages. It's time to Pay Our Providers.

Our demands for New York State's budget

Two investments. One promise kept.

$1.8B

A wage scale for child care workers

Guarantee thriving wages for all child care providers throughout New York State, whether in center or residential settings, without changing what families pay.

The details
  • Pay follows the scale built by the NYS Early Childhood Education Workforce Collaborative, led by CUNY's Professional Development Institute, based on role, experience, credentials and competencies.
  • It's grounded in parity with public school teachers in local public school systems because child care providers are educators. Credentials like a CDA, associate's, bachelor's, and others help providers advance -- but experience alone counts for pay increases too.
  • Family child care providers start 10% above the teacher scale, because they run a business as well as teach. Assistants start 10% below. Directors are benchmarked to assistant principal and principal pay.
  • Raises can't come out of parents' pockets: no tuition increases for private-pay families, and no interference with collective bargaining.
  • The $1.8B is the cost when fully phased in.

$1.04B

CCAP for every eligible family

Providers and families alike depend on the Child Care Assistance Program. It must be fully funded to end waitlists and ensure providers have fair, reliable income.

The details
  • $400 million statewide and $640 million for New York City in new recurring funding, so no eligible family is turned away or waitlisted, and children with special needs can get the care they're entitled to.
  • In May 2026, 31 counties plus New York City had stopped enrolling eligible families because the money ran out. NYC's waitlist reached about 30,000 children before the City began moving 10,000 off it in October.
  • Running CCAP for the 57 counties outside NYC through the State, rather than county by county, so a family's chance at care doesn't depend on its zip code.
  • For providers, fully funded CCAP means classrooms at capacity and payments they can count on.

Grounded in the policy principles refined by hundreds of providers and policy advocates over many months:

On September 28, 2026, providers, parents, unions, advocates, researchers and elected officials met at the first New York State Child Care Alignment Table (NYCAT). Our 2027 demands are the first steps toward them.

  1. A statewide compensation standard and career ladder. A minimum compensation structure and career ladder for the entire child care workforce, with a wage scale like the CUNY PDI and New Mexico models, indexed to the cost of living.The $1.8B wage scale
  2. Subsidies and payment reform. Payment pathways, including pay subsidies and CCAP rates based on the true cost of care, so providers are paid on the wage scale without absorbing the cost themselves.A fully funded CCAP
  3. Worker voice and bargaining. Providers in every setting, statewide, with the power to advance and enforce measures that secure pay justice, by leveraging tools like collective bargaining, wage floors, and more.Our organizing
  4. Sustainable financing. A responsible phase-in of the wage scale, backed by a statewide progressive revenue package dedicated to it until child care is universal.Both demands

01The NeedPoverty wages, empty classrooms, families turned away

Providers are paid like their work doesn't matter

  • $37,675 a year. That's the median for a New York child care educator, less than 96% of occupations in the state, and below retail salespeople ($37,848). An elementary school teacher's median is $84,721. Schuyler Center, Child Care Workforce 2026 (NYS Dept. of Labor data)
  • $25,000 a year was the median income of NYC's 32,917 child care workers in 2023, 45% of what other NYC workers earn. 93% are women and 87% are people of color. NYC Comptroller, Child Care Affordability and the Benefits of Universal Provision
  • $6.01 an hour is the median take-home pay of NYC's licensed home-based providers, once they account for the costs of providing child care in their homes. Nearly all earn less than the minimum wage. Lauren Melodia, Center for New York City Affairs at The New School, budget testimony, Feb 2026
  • At least 65% of child care workers rely on some form of public assistance, and nearly 40% rely on Medicaid for health coverage. OCFS Child Care Availability Task Force, 2024; Schuyler Center
  • A certified teacher who stays in a community program instead of moving to a public school gives up about $1.7 million over a 25-year career. Day Care Council of New York, Building a Stronger Child Care Workforce, Dec 2025

So the people leave, and the care goes with them

  • 57% of New York programs are short-staffed, leaving more than 16,000 children unserved because positions sit empty. Day Care Council of New York, Dec 2025
  • 766 classrooms in centers and afterschool programs were fully closed for lack of staff. Empire State Campaign for Child Care and Schuyler Center staffing survey, Mar 2023
  • Family child care capacity fell 28% from 2019 to 2024. Home-based providers made up 84% of all provider losses statewide. Schuyler Center (OCFS data); NYS Comptroller Thomas P. DiNapoli, Feb 2025
  • 60% of New York's census tracts are child care deserts. NYS Comptroller, Lingering Challenges in the Child Care Sector, Feb 2025

And families can't get in the door

  • Child care in New York averages $14,621 a year per child, the second-highest in the nation. Infant care in a center averages $20,459. NYS Comptroller, Feb 2025
  • In May 2026, 31 counties and New York City had stopped enrolling eligible families in child care assistance because funds ran out. The state budget added $1.2 billion for CCAP but nothing for workers' wages. New York Focus, June 2026
  • NYC's voucher waitlist reached about 30,000 children this fall. Thanks to organizing by POP and its allies, the City began moving 10,000 children off it in October. Thousands are still waiting. Chalkbeat, Oct 2026
02The SolutionA real wage scale, a funded CCAP, and the revenue to sustain them

A wage scale that treats early educators as educators

The scale, developed by the NYS Early Childhood Education Workforce Collaborative and CUNY-PDI, starts from a simple idea: caring for and teaching a two-year-old is as important as teaching a ten-year-old, in any setting. Here's what it would mean for real New Yorkers:

ProviderEarns nowWith the scale
Family child care educator in NYC, high school diploma, 21 years$33,280$79,184
Teacher in NYC, associate degree in ECE, 3 years$40,000$69,836
Family child care provider in Buffalo, high school diploma, 11 years$33,280$53,792
Center director in Rochester, bachelor's degree and administrator credential, 6 years$52,000$76,022

Source: NYS Early Childhood Education Workforce Collaborative / CUNY-PDI wage scale scenarios.

Full funding for child care assistance

$400 million statewide and $640 million for New York City, recurring, so no eligible family is waitlisted and providers can count on full classrooms and steady payments. A funded CCAP is how a wage scale reaches the providers who serve working families.

New York can afford it

  • The Invest in Our New York coalition's four bills would raise more than $40 billion a year by asking the wealthiest New Yorkers and the most profitable corporations to pay their share: new income tax brackets for the top 5% of earners ($21 billion), a higher rate for corporations with more than $5 million in New York profits ($5 billion), a surcharge on large capital gains ($12 billion), and a tax on inheritances over $250,000 ($4 billion). Sen. Jabari Brisport and Asm. Anna Kelles, who stood with providers at NYCAT, sponsor two of them. Invest in Our New York, testimony to the Legislature's revenue hearing, Feb 2026; investinourny.org
  • The Fiscal Policy Institute shows a narrower package, including a small payroll tax and higher taxes on corporations and on incomes over $1 million, would raise about $8 billion a year: enough for universal child care statewide with providers earning $60,000 in NYC and $50,000 elsewhere. Fiscal Policy Institute, A Tax Plan for Statewide Universal Childcare, Dec 2025
  • Every $1 invested in high-quality care from birth to age five returns $7.30 over a child's life, through better health, schooling and earnings for children and higher earnings for their parents. James Heckman et al., The Life-cycle Benefits of an Influential Early Childhood Program (NBER); University of Chicago summary
  • New York would see an estimated $3.2 billion in additional wages from parents who could go to work once child care is substantially expanded and robustly funded. The Century Foundation, cited in NYC Comptroller, Child Care Affordability and the Benefits of Universal Provision
  • When Washington, DC raised early educators' pay by $10,000 to $14,000 through its Pay Equity Fund, turnover dropped. Mathematica; AQE and Empire State Campaign for Child Care testimony, Feb 2026

Researchers including Lauren Melodia (Center for New York City Affairs) and the Day Care Council of New York also call for a sector-wide salary scale, a true cost-of-care standard in place of the market-rate survey, and a wage fund to bridge the gap.
